Gentle Giants

a celebration of manatees — for anyone who already loves them

Manatees don't do fast, and they don't do fierce. They spend their days drifting through warm, shallow water, working through dozens of kinds of plants, mostly minding their own business — which might be exactly why they're so easy to underestimate. They're closer kin to elephants than to any other creature in the sea, they remember things for years at a time, and they raise their calves with a tenderness that's hard to watch and not fall for. This page is for anyone who already has.

Meet the Gentle Giant

Enormous, Unhurried, Entirely Harmless

An adult manatee is a genuinely huge animal doing a very unhurried impression of a much smaller one — typically around 3 metres long and 800 to 1,200 pounds, with the largest ever recorded pushing 15 feet and close to 3,900 pounds. All of that mass runs on salad: manatees are strict herbivores, working through more than 60 species of seagrass and freshwater plants, and can spend up to eight hours a day grazing to take in somewhere between 4 and 10 percent of their own body weight in vegetation.

That size buys them something almost no other large wild animal gets: manatees have no natural predators at all. The only real threat they've ever faced is us — mostly boat strikes and cold water, never teeth.

Cousins of the Elephant

Closer Kin Than You'd Guess

Their closest living relatives aren't dolphins or seals — they're elephants. The resemblance shows up in oddly specific places: female manatees have just two teats, tucked under the front flippers rather than on the chest, exactly where an elephant's are, and manatee flippers still carry small vestigial toenails — a quiet leftover from four legs and dry land, tens of millions of years back, when both branches of the family were still land animals.

The teeth make the case just as well. Manatees have what researchers call "marching molars": new teeth form at the back of the jaw and slowly migrate forward, about a millimetre a month, replacing the ones wearing down and dropping out at the front — a literal conveyor belt that runs for the animal's whole 50-to-60-year life. It's a trick shared with exactly one other living mammal: the elephant, though an elephant only gets six sets across a lifetime, where a manatee's molars just keep on coming.

Smarter Than She Looks

A Very Good Memory, Undersold

Manatees have a famously smooth-looking brain and a reputation, pretty much entirely undeserved, for being simple animals. Researchers at Florida's Mote Marine Laboratory ran manatees through texture- and shape-discrimination tasks, then tested them again 14 and 29 months later — the animals still performed at 100 percent accuracy, one of the longer documented memory-retention results recorded in any non-primate mammal.

In the wild, that memory shows up as detailed spatial knowledge: manatees return to the same warm-water refuges and feeding grounds year after year, and they've been observed playing — riding boat wakes, gently mouthing objects, play-wrestling with each other — behaviour usually associated with more obviously "clever" animals. Their other quiet superpower is touch: manatees are covered, not just around the face, in hundreds of sparse whiskers, used to feel out food, current, and the shape of the world around them.

The Calves

GESTATION ~12–13 MONTHS
BIRTH WEIGHT 60–70 LBS
NURSING UP TO 2 YEARS

A manatee pregnancy lasts around 12 to 13 months and almost always produces a single calf — twins happen, but rarely. A newborn is already substantial: 3 to 4 feet long and 60 to 70 pounds, and within minutes of being born it makes its first swim, straight up to the surface for its first breath, usually with its mother's help.

Nursing happens underwater, from two teats tucked behind the front flippers — not so different from where an elephant calf would look for milk. Calves keep drinking for up to two years, but they start experimenting with seagrass within just weeks of being born, learning to eat solid food while still nursing, much the way any toddler does. In murky water, where they can lose sight of each other in a few inches, mother and calf keep track of one another by squeaking and chirping constantly — a running conversation that never really stops.

That two-year bond is close and constant: calves stay with their mothers the whole time, learning the migration routes, the feeding grounds, and the warm-water refuges they'll rely on for the rest of their lives. It's also exactly why manatee mothers usually only have a new calf every two to five years — raising one properly is close to a full-time job.

— odd and wonderful —

A handful of facts that don't fit neatly anywhere else, but earn their place regardless:

Three Species, One Nickname

There are three manatee species — the West Indian manatee (which splits into a Florida and an Antillean subspecies), the Amazonian manatee (the smallest, and the only one that never touches salt water), and the West African manatee. All three, plus their more distant cousin the dugong, get called "sea cows" for the unhurried grazing habit. The dugong is the odd one out of the group — a different family entirely, easily told apart by its whale-like fluked tail, versus a manatee's round paddle.

The Gas Nuance

Manatees can hover motionless at almost any depth without paddling, and part of the reason is genuinely a little silly: a high-fibre diet produces a lot of intestinal gas, and holding onto some of it in gut pouches adds useful lift. That said, the serious engineering behind it is a nearly full-body diaphragm and unusually dense, heavy ribs that work like adjustable ballast — the gas is real, but it's the supporting act, not the headliner.

Feel the Cold

Manatees carry very little body fat and can't tolerate water much below about 68°F (20°C) — prolonged cold exposure can cause a genuinely dangerous condition called cold stress syndrome. Every winter, Florida's manatees migrate to natural springs and warm-water outflows to sit the season out, which is why so many of the best manatee-watching spots are built around a spring or a power station.

The Original Mermaid

One of the earliest written mermaid sightings is often traced to Christopher Columbus's 1493 journal, describing three "mermaids" off the coast of Hispaniola that were, in his words, not half as beautiful as they're painted — almost certainly manatees, seen at a distance by someone who'd been at sea a very long time.
