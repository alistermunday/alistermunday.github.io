Roads Lined With Trees

a small tour of the world's great avenues

Every country seems to have at least one road where the trees took over — planted deliberately, decades or centuries ago, by someone who wouldn't live to see the canopy close. Starting close to home, then further out.

United Kingdom

The Dark Hedges

Bregagh Road, near Armoy, County Antrim, Northern Ireland

An avenue of beech trees planted in the 1770s by the Stuart family, meant to impress visitors arriving at their Georgian mansion, Gracehill House. Two and a half centuries later the branches have grown together into a solid tunnel overhead. Became globally famous as the "Kingsroad" in Game of Thrones — before that it was a quiet local backroad most people outside Antrim had never heard of.

Photo: Wikimedia Commons

Lime Tree Avenue, Clumber Park

Clumber Park, Nottinghamshire

Two miles long, roughly 1,300 common limes planted in the 1840s along the old carriage drive to a stately home that no longer exists (it burned down in 1938 and was demolished). The avenue outlived the house it was built to lead to, and is now reckoned the longest double avenue of its kind in Europe. National Trust land today.

Photo: Wikimedia Commons
Europe

Tunnel of Love

Klevan, Rivne Oblast, Ukraine

Not a road at all, technically — a private industrial railway spur linking a fibreboard factory to the rail network — but the trees either side have grown into a three-mile green tunnel around the track. A train passes only a few times a day, which is exactly why couples walk it: quiet enough, and green enough, that it earned its nickname on its own, with no tourist board involved.

Photo: Wikimedia Commons

Deutsche Alleenstraße

Rügen (Baltic coast) to Lake Constance — the whole length of Germany

Not one avenue but a signposted route stitching together the best of them: roughly 2,900 km of tree-lined road, oak, lime, chestnut and poplar, much of it dating back to 19th-century Prussian road-building (trees were planted partly for shade, partly to mark the road edge in snow). Germany has an entire preservation society devoted to keeping these avenues from being felled for road-widening.

Photo: Wikimedia Commons

Cours Mirabeau

Aix-en-Provence, France

A 440-metre boulevard laid out in the 1650s where the town's fortifications used to stand, double-planted with plane trees that now form a continuous shaded arch — deliberately designed as a promenade rather than a thoroughfare. Cézanne, who grew up in Aix, walked it most days of his life.

Photo: Wikimedia Commons
International

Avenue of Oaks, Boone Hall

Mount Pleasant, South Carolina, USA

Live oaks planted in 1743, three-quarters of a mile long, draped floor to canopy in Spanish moss (which is neither Spanish nor a moss — an air plant that just hangs off the branches). Took nearly 90 years before the canopy fully closed overhead. One of the most photographed plantation approaches in the American South, and one of the oldest deliberately planted oak avenues anywhere.

Photo: Wikimedia Commons

Icho Namiki — the Ginkgo Avenue

Meiji Jingu Gaien, Tokyo, Japan

About 150 ginkgo trees planted in the 1920s leading up to the Meiji Memorial Picture Gallery, trimmed for a century into a deliberate pyramid shape — taller in the centre, shorter at the edges, so the whole avenue reads as one continuous form rather than a row of individual trees. For two or three weeks each November it turns solid gold and half of Tokyo turns up to see it.
