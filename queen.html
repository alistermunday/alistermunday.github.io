QUEEN

1970 — forever

Four musicians who refused to sound like anyone else — opera, glam, rock, disco, funk, all stitched together with total confidence and zero shame. No genre ever held them for more than one album.

The Four

Freddie Mercury
Vocals, piano, showman-in-chief
Born Farrokh Bulsara, 5 Sept 1946, Stone Town, Zanzibar — Parsi-Indian family, sent to boarding school in India where he trained classically on piano and formed his first band. Died 24 Nov 1991.

The voice everyone remembers, but he was also the band's visual identity — he studied graphic design at Ealing Art College and designed Queen's crest himself: the zodiac signs of all four members (two lions for Leo, a crab for Cancer, two fairies for Virgo) woven around a phoenix, with a Q for a crown. Four-octave range, and famously refused to have his prominent front teeth fixed his whole life, convinced it was part of what gave his voice its resonance.

The contradiction people who knew him always mention: the strutting, command-72,000-people persona was almost entirely a stage creation. Offstage he was shy, read voraciously, and was known among friends as generous to the point of secrecy about it. Wrote "Bohemian Rhapsody," "Killer Queen," "Somebody to Love," "Don't Stop Me Now," "Crazy Little Thing Called Love" — a songwriting range from mock-opera to rockabilly pastiche that no one else in the band came close to matching. Kept recording vocals for what became "Made in Heaven" right up until his voice gave out, knowing he was dying.

Brian May
Guitar
Born 19 July 1947, Hampton, Middlesex. Also: Dr. Brian May — PhD in astrophysics, Imperial College London.

Started a physics degree, then a PhD on interplanetary dust and the zodiacal light — paused it in 1974 when Queen took off, and quietly went back and finished it in 2007, more than three decades later. Before Queen he and Roger Taylor were in a band called Smile; when their singer left, a friend of a friend named Freddie joined and told them to change the name. Built his own guitar, the Red Special, out of a fireplace mantel and bicycle parts — full story further down.

The quiet one of the loud sounds: soft-spoken, professorial in interviews, and the guitarist behind some of the most aggressive tones in classic rock. Long-running animal welfare campaigner — co-founded the Save Me Trust, campaigned hard against fox hunting and the UK badger cull. There's an asteroid named after him, 52665 Brianmay, and he played a guitar solo live from the roof of Buckingham Palace for the Queen's Golden Jubilee in 2002.

Roger Taylor
Drums, high harmonies
Born Roger Meddows Taylor, 26 July 1949, Dartford (raised in Norfolk/Cornwall).

Started a dentistry course, switched to a biology degree, finished neither — music won. That scream in "Bohemian Rhapsody" is his; so is most of the piercing high harmony stack that makes Queen's choruses sound like a cathedral choir rather than a rock band. The drummer stereotype (just keeps time, doesn't write) never applied to him: "Radio Ga Ga," "A Kind of Magic," and co-writes on "Innuendo" and "The Show Must Go On" are all his.

Reputation as the band's sharpest wit in interviews — dry, cheeky, least precious about the band's legacy of the three survivors. Ran a parallel side project, The Cross, through the early 90s, and kept a solo career going the whole time. Still tours today as Queen + Adam Lambert.

John Deacon
Bass
Born 19 August 1951, Leicester. Last to join — 1971, after answering a notice-board audition call.

Studied electronics at Chelsea College and put it to direct use: built his own amplifier from scavenged parts — the "Deacy Amp" — which shows up all over Queen's records for its particular warm, saturated tone, including Brian May's multi-tracked "orchestral" guitar passages. Self-taught on bass, and quietly responsible for some of the band's biggest commercial peaks: "Another One Bites the Dust" (a bassline he wrote after getting into Chic — became Queen's best-selling US single), "I Want to Break Free," "You're My Best Friend."

By far the most private of the four. Played on 1997's "No-One But You" tribute after Freddie's death, then stepped back from music and public life almost entirely — no interviews, no reunion performances, essentially no public appearances since. Of the three surviving members, he's the one nobody has a recent photo of.

The Red Special

Brian May live with the Red Special — the same guitar since 1964. (Wikimedia Commons)

Brian couldn't afford a real Fender, Gibson, or Höfner, so in August 1963 he and his father Harold started building one instead — evenings and weekends, over the next fourteen months, finishing in October 1964. Brian still plays that exact guitar today. It's nicknamed "the Fireplace" and "the Old Lady."

What it's actually made of

Neck: a 100-year-old mahogany fireplace mantel a family friend was about to bin
Fretboard: oak, painted black, worm holes filled with matchsticks
Body: blockboard with oak inserts from an old table, faced in mahogany veneer to look solid
Fret markers: hand-shaped mother-of-pearl buttons
Edging: white plastic shelf trim, to stop the edges chipping
Total cost: about £17.45 — nearly everything was scavenged from around the house

The tremolo — the clever bit

Brian wanted dive-bombs that snapped back perfectly in tune, so he engineered a knife-edge tremolo from an old hardened-steel blade, tensioned by two motorbike valve springs fighting the strings' 79 lb of pull. The arm itself is a bicycle saddlebag-holder, tipped with a piece of plastic knitting needle. The bridge rollers were hand-shaped by Brian with a drill, sandpaper, and a file so the strings glide back to pitch with zero friction.

Sound

Three Burns Tri-Sonic pickups, each with its own on/off and phase switch — giving Brian a huge range of tones from one guitar without ever touching a pedal. He picks with an old English sixpence coin instead of a plectrum, for a brighter, harder attack. He built it deliberately to feed back and "sing," after watching Jeff Beck get sounds just by moving his guitar near the amp.

Why it matters

Virtually every guitar part on every Queen record — "Bohemian Rhapsody," "We Will Rock You," all of it — is this one homemade instrument. A guitar a teenager and his dad built for the cost of a few pub rounds ended up defining the sound of one of the biggest bands in the world.

Turning Points

1975 — "Bohemian Rhapsody" released. Six minutes, no chorus, an operatic middle section, and a record label told them it would never get radio play. It became one of the best-selling singles in UK history.
1977 — "We Will Rock You" / "We Are the Champions." Written deliberately as songs the crowd could sing back. It worked; it's still working.
1980 — "Another One Bites the Dust" — a bassline Deacon wrote after listening to Chic, and Queen's biggest US hit.
13 July 1985 — Live Aid, Wembley. Twenty minutes that are still taught as the definition of how to command a stadium. "Ay-oh" call and response with 72,000 people. Widely rated the greatest live performance in rock history.
24 November 1991 — Freddie Mercury died of AIDS-related bronchopneumonia, one day after publicly confirming his diagnosis. He kept recording vocals until his voice gave out.
