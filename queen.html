QUEEN

1970 — forever

Four musicians who refused to sound like anyone else — opera, glam, rock, disco, funk, all stitched together with total confidence and zero shame. No genre ever held them for more than one album.

The Four

Freddie Mercury
Vocals, piano, showman-in-chief

Four-octave range, a piano he could make sound like a cathedral, and a stage presence no one has matched since. Wrote "Bohemian Rhapsody," "Killer Queen," "Somebody to Love," "Don't Stop Me Now."

Brian May
Guitar

Built his own guitar, the Red Special, out of a fireplace mantel and bicycle parts. That homemade guitar is the sound of nearly every Queen record. Astrophysicist on the side. Full story below ↓

Roger Taylor
Drums, high harmonies

That scream in "Bohemian Rhapsody" — his. Wrote "Radio Ga Ga" and "A Kind of Magic."

John Deacon
Bass

Quietest one, wrote some of the biggest hooks anyway — "Another One Bites the Dust," "I Want to Break Free." Retired from public life after Freddie's death.

The Red Special

Brian May live with the Red Special — the same guitar since 1964. (Wikimedia Commons)

Brian couldn't afford a real Fender, Gibson, or Höfner, so in August 1963 he and his father Harold started building one instead — evenings and weekends, over the next fourteen months, finishing in October 1964. Brian still plays that exact guitar today. It's nicknamed "the Fireplace" and "the Old Lady."

What it's actually made of

Neck: a 100-year-old mahogany fireplace mantel a family friend was about to bin
Fretboard: oak, painted black, worm holes filled with matchsticks
Body: blockboard with oak inserts from an old table, faced in mahogany veneer to look solid
Fret markers: hand-shaped mother-of-pearl buttons
Edging: white plastic shelf trim, to stop the edges chipping
Total cost: about £17.45 — nearly everything was scavenged from around the house

The tremolo — the clever bit

Brian wanted dive-bombs that snapped back perfectly in tune, so he engineered a knife-edge tremolo from an old hardened-steel blade, tensioned by two motorbike valve springs fighting the strings' 79 lb of pull. The arm itself is a bicycle saddlebag-holder, tipped with a piece of plastic knitting needle. The bridge rollers were hand-shaped by Brian with a drill, sandpaper, and a file so the strings glide back to pitch with zero friction.

Sound

Three Burns Tri-Sonic pickups, each with its own on/off and phase switch — giving Brian a huge range of tones from one guitar without ever touching a pedal. He picks with an old English sixpence coin instead of a plectrum, for a brighter, harder attack. He built it deliberately to feed back and "sing," after watching Jeff Beck get sounds just by moving his guitar near the amp.

Why it matters

Virtually every guitar part on every Queen record — "Bohemian Rhapsody," "We Will Rock You," all of it — is this one homemade instrument. A guitar a teenager and his dad built for the cost of a few pub rounds ended up defining the sound of one of the biggest bands in the world.

Turning Points

1975 — "Bohemian Rhapsody" released. Six minutes, no chorus, an operatic middle section, and a record label told them it would never get radio play. It became one of the best-selling singles in UK history.
1977 — "We Will Rock You" / "We Are the Champions." Written deliberately as songs the crowd could sing back. It worked; it's still working.
1980 — "Another One Bites the Dust" — a bassline Deacon wrote after listening to Chic, and Queen's biggest US hit.
13 July 1985 — Live Aid, Wembley. Twenty minutes that are still taught as the definition of how to command a stadium. "Ay-oh" call and response with 72,000 people. Widely rated the greatest live performance in rock history.
24 November 1991 — Freddie Mercury died of AIDS-related bronchopneumonia, one day after publicly confirming his diagnosis. He kept recording vocals until his voice gave out.
